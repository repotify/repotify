Let me think about what the design actually gets right:

1. **Three-arm comparison with proper control**: A (repotify) vs B (none) vs C (naive, same size as A). The naive arm is the crucial one — it controls for "any skills help" vs "the *right* skills help." Many eval designs skip this and overclaim. Same-size seeding controls for prompt-length/context effects. This isolates the recommender's signal from mere skill injection.

2. **Routing recall as a separable metric**: They decompose the question into (a) can the agent pick the right skills, and (b) do the right skills improve the deliverable. This separates the routing problem from the end-task problem. If recall is high but success doesn't move, the skill cards or summaries are the problem, not the recommender. That's a diagnostic, not just a scoreboard.

3. **Deterministic rubric, no LLM judge**: Removes judge variance, judge bias, judge cost. Rubric checks (contains/regex/not-contains) are reproducible. Shallow, yes — but shallow and *knowable* beats deep and noisy at pilot scale. You can diff two runs and know the delta is real signal, not judge drift.

4. **Cheap, fast, repeatable**: Single-turn, one call, --runs N, JSONL append. You can run 100 reps overnight. Statistical power comes from reps, not per-run sophistication. With 2 reps default and cheap runs, scaling reps is trivial.

5. **Model-agnostic driver registry + mock driver**: You can test the harness itself deterministically (mock), and swap models via config. The Jev decision-model arm lets you test routing signal without chat noise. This means the harness outlives GLM-5.3.

6. **Honest accounting of limits**: They list the known limits themselves. Estimated tokens flagged as estimated. Non-determinism treated as random draws — which is the correct statistical frame: reps are samples, you report distributions, not single runs.

What real decisions will the numbers support?

- Go/no-go on investing more in the recommender pipeline.
- Whether skill cards' 160-char summaries are informative enough for routing (recall tells you directly).
- Whether to build multi-turn/tool-use agent v2 or fix routing first.
- Which scenarios the recommender helps in (per-scenario breakdown) — where to focus.
- Whether the delta A vs C justifies the pipeline cost vs just seeding random skills (if naive ≈ repotify, the recommender is dead weight).

Why is this the right v1 scope?

- Single-turn isolates the variable of interest: skill selection. Multi-turn agents confound skill effect with planning/tool-use variance. You can't attribute deltas in a multi-step agent with 5 scenarios and 2 reps.
- Small scope = actually ships. The failure mode of eval harnesses is overbuilding before you have a signal.
- The JSONL/telemetry schema merge means data accumulates comparably across versions — you're building a time series, not a one-off benchmark.
- Everything cheap is automated; everything expensive (LLM judge, multi-turn) is deferred until the cheap version shows signal worth measuring precisely.
